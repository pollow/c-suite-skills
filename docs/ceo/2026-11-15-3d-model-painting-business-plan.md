# Business Plan: Hand-Painted 3D Models

**Market:** India  
**Stage:** Concept / validation  
**Planning horizon:** First 90 days

## Executive decision

Start as a small, appointment-based commission service for hand-painting customer-supplied 3D-printed miniatures and figures. Coordinate printing through a local print partner only when customers need it; do not buy a 3D printer until paid demand and repeatable order economics are demonstrated.

The initial advantage is careful hand-finishing, clear communication, and reliable delivery—not owning more equipment. Demand and the prices below are hypotheses to test, not verified market data.

## Customer and offer

Start locally, then accept domestic shipments once packing and delivery have been tested. Prioritize tabletop gamers and miniature collectors who want a ready-to-use painted model. Secondary customers can include gift buyers and independent sculptors who need painted portfolio samples.

| Offer | Initial test price (INR) | Scope |
|---|---:|---|
| Simple tabletop miniature | ₹600–₹1,200 | Basic preparation, base colors, wash, and protective finish |
| Detailed miniature or small figure | ₹1,500–₹3,500 | More color transitions and detail; quote from photos |
| Display figure or bust | ₹3,500–₹8,000+ | Custom quote based on size, condition, and detail |
| Print coordination | Quoted separately | Outsourced print cost plus handling; never silently bundled |

These are launch-test bands, not established Indian market rates. Adjust for size, surface condition, assembly, paint scheme, turnaround, and shipping. Publish example photos and a clear “what’s included” list before taking orders.

## Sales and positioning

Position the service as **“your print, finished by hand and ready to display or play.”** Show unpainted-to-finished comparisons, close-up detail, and actual turnaround times.

1. Make 5–6 original or properly licensed sample pieces.
2. Share a small portfolio through Instagram and WhatsApp Business; take inquiries with a short photo-and-size form.
3. Ask local 3D-print shops, hobby groups, tabletop communities, and makerspaces about referrals or sample displays.
4. Request permission to photograph completed work and ask satisfied customers for a review or referral.
5. Avoid paid ads and marketplace subscriptions until the first customers reveal which channel converts.

Do not advertise or accept work that infringes someone else’s intellectual property. Confirm rights for commercial sculpts and branded characters, and check local consumer, tax, and business requirements with an Indian accountant or relevant professional before scaling.

## Order and fulfillment workflow

1. Customer submits model photos, dimensions, material, desired finish, reference images, and deadline.
2. Confirm the model is paintable; quote labor, any repair/assembly, outsourced printing, packing, and shipping separately.
3. Take a 50% booking deposit; state the expected completion window and revision/return terms in writing.
4. Photograph the model at intake, prepare and paint it, then send progress photos if the job is complex.
5. Get approval on the finished photos, collect the balance, apply the protective finish, and pack securely.
6. Share tracking details and request feedback after delivery.

Begin with a limited number of orders per week. Keep a job log for quoted price, actual painting hours, consumables, rework, shipping, delivery time, and customer source. This log—not impressions—should drive pricing and any equipment purchase.

## Lean financial model

Use the following as a planning example for one average order; replace every input with actual pilot data:

| Per-order assumption | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Average selling price | ₹2,500 |
| Paint, packaging, payment fees, and rework reserve (25%) | ₹625 |
| Founder labor (4 hours × ₹250/hour) | ₹1,000 |
| Contribution after these costs | **₹875** |

At this example, 10, 20, and 30 orders per month mean ₹25,000, ₹50,000, and ₹75,000 in sales, with ₹8,750, ₹17,500, and ₹26,250 respectively after the modeled direct costs and labor, but **before fixed overhead and tax**. These are scenarios, not a forecast or take-home income. Break-even orders = monthly fixed costs ÷ actual contribution per order.

Keep the initial setup limited to essential hand tools, paints, lighting, and packing supplies already affordable to the founder. Get local supplier quotes before setting a startup budget. Defer printer purchase until the service has at least 10 paid jobs, repeatable positive contribution after valuing labor, and evidence that customers are being lost because printing is unavailable.

## 90-day validation plan

| Timing | Work | Evidence to continue |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Interview 15 likely customers and 3 print shops; make 5–6 portfolio samples; collect supplier and shipping quotes | At least 5 people request a quote and at least 2 print shops show referral interest |
| Weeks 3–4 | Complete 5 paid pilot jobs; record time and all direct costs | Customers pay the test price; actual time/costs are recorded for every job |
| Month 2 | Refine the menu and quote rules; request reviews; follow up with pilot customers | At least 2 referrals or repeat inquiries, with acceptable rework and on-time delivery |
| Month 3 | Run a second cohort; compare channels, prices, and hourly contribution | Decide to continue, narrow the offer, or stop before making a major equipment investment |

Use these as decision gates, not guarantees. If people praise samples but do not pay deposits, change the offer or target segment before spending more.

## Main risks and controls

- **Unproven willingness to pay:** Require deposits and test paid commissions before buying equipment.
- **Underquoted labor:** Track actual hours, charge separately for repairs and complexity, and revise prices after the first five jobs.
- **Shipping damage or quality disputes:** Photograph intake and completion, test packaging, and state the handling and revision policy before accepting payment.
- **Unreliable print quality:** Inspect outsourced prints before accepting them; quote cleanup or replacement separately.
- **Copyright and compliance exposure:** Use original/licensed sculpts and obtain professional guidance on applicable Indian tax, consumer, and business requirements.
- **Founder capacity:** Cap weekly slots and quote realistic lead times; do not promise rush work until the workflow is repeatable.

## Founder decisions

Before launch, choose the first city/community to serve, confirm painting skill and available tools, and decide whether the first offer will be limited to customer-supplied models. The existing company profile describes a separate open-source software project; this plan treats 3D-model painting as a separate venture and does not change that profile.
